The biggest difference I noticed was not food, cars or the size of everything. It was how people get things moving.

In France, we are taught to be thorough. Before we commit to something, we want to understand it, question it, and make sure the facts are right. We ask a lot of questions first.

Americans work on momentum. They get into action fast, and getting something done matters more than getting it perfect on the first try. That does not mean they are vague about it. They are very direct about what they are going to do. When an American presents something, they will often open with "I am going to cover this, this and this," and then do exactly that.

If you have read Erin Meyer's *The Culture Map*, this is her persuading scale. France sits near the "principles first" end: you build the reasoning and the theory, and the conclusion comes at the end. The US sits near the "applications first" end: you start with the conclusion and what to do about it, and you add the reasoning only if someone needs it.

Neither is wrong. But if you do not know about it, the American way can feel rushed or shallow, and the French way can come across as slow, negative or overly critical. Americans may read your questions as doubt when you meant them as diligence.

## What helps

* Lead with your point, then explain. Say what you think or what you propose first.
* When you ask a lot of questions, say why ("I want to make sure I get this right") so it does not sound like pushback.
* Do not mistake speed for carelessness. Things get adjusted along the way.

## The friendliness is real

The same energy shows up socially. Americans are quick to start a conversation, quick to be warm, quick to include you. A lot of French people assume this friendliness is fake. In my experience it is not. People really are friendly.
